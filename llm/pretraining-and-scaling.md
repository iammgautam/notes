You will almost certainly never pretrain a frontier model, but pretraining decides what every model you use knows, what it does by default, and where it fails. Understanding it lets you reason about knowledge cutoffs, why base and instruct models behave so differently, why a small model trained on huge amounts of data can beat a larger one, how much a fine-tuning run will cost, and why a benchmark score might be meaningless. This note covers the objective, the data, the compute math, the scaling laws, and the post-training stages that turn a base model into an assistant.

## Core ideas

### The objective: predict the next token

Pretraining minimizes cross-entropy on next-token prediction over a huge corpus. For a sequence of tokens `t1 ... tn`, the loss is the average of `-log p(t_i | t_1 ... t_(i-1))` over every position. With causal masking, one forward pass scores every position at once, so each token in the corpus is a training example (see [Transformer architecture]({{ '/llm/transformer-architecture/' | relative_url }})). Training uses teacher forcing: the model always conditions on the true prefix, never on its own earlier guesses.

The objective is simple, but predicting text well requires modeling whatever produced the text: grammar, facts, the structure of code, arithmetic, the intent behind an argument. Capabilities are a byproduct of driving this loss down at scale. There are no labels; the text supervises itself.

Two implementation details show up later in fine-tuning:

- **Packing.** Short documents are concatenated into fixed-length training sequences separated by end-of-document tokens, so no compute is wasted on padding. Some setups mask attention across document boundaries.
- **Loss curves.** Loss falls fast, then slowly, following a power law. Sudden spikes usually mean instability (learning rate, bad data, numerical issues) rather than learning.

### Data is the main lever

Architectures across labs are now similar; data quality, quantity and mixture explain much of the difference between models. A typical pipeline:

```text
web crawl, code, books, papers, math, curated and licensed sources
   │
   ▼
extract text (HTML, PDF) ─► language identification
   │
   ▼
heuristic filters: length, symbol ratio, repetition, boilerplate
   │
   ▼
quality classifiers ─► deduplication (exact hashes, MinHash near-dups)
   │
   ▼
PII and toxicity filtering ─► benchmark decontamination
   │
   ▼
tokenize ─► sample by domain mixture weights ─► training stream
```

- **Filtering.** Raw web crawl is mostly navigation menus, spam, duplicated boilerplate and machine-generated text. Heuristic rules remove the worst; model-based quality classifiers (for example, scoring pages for educational value) select the best. Aggressive quality filtering has repeatedly improved models trained on fewer tokens.
- **Deduplication.** Duplicate documents waste compute and increase memorization of the repeated content. Near-duplicate detection with MinHash over n-grams is standard.
- **Mixture.** The final stream samples domains (web, code, math, books, multilingual, academic) with tuned weights. Upweighting code and math improves reasoning-style tasks well beyond programming. Mixture weights are chosen with small proxy runs.
- **Scale.** Reported pretraining corpora for recent open models are on the order of 10–40 trillion tokens, as of 2025–2026. High-quality text is finite, so labs repeat their best data for several epochs and add synthetic data generated by earlier models.

The data also fixes the knowledge cutoff. Anything after the crawl date, or absent from the crawl (your company's documents), is unknown to the model and has to come through the prompt; see [RAG architecture]({{ '/llm/rag-architecture/' | relative_url }}).

### Compute: the 6ND rule

Training compute for a dense transformer is approximately:

```text
C ≈ 6 × N × D        FLOPs
N = parameters, D = training tokens
2ND for the forward pass, 4ND for the backward pass
```

Inference costs about `2N` FLOPs per token. Converting FLOPs to GPU time needs the hardware's peak throughput and the fraction you actually achieve, model FLOPs utilization (MFU). Well-tuned large training runs commonly report MFU in the 30–50% range.

```text
GPU-hours = C / (peak FLOP/s per GPU × MFU) / 3600
```

### Scaling laws

Kaplan et al. (2020) showed that language model loss falls as a smooth power law in parameters, data and compute across many orders of magnitude. That predictability is what makes billion-dollar runs plannable: fit curves on small runs, extrapolate the loss of the big one.

Hoffmann et al. (2022), the Chinchilla paper, asked how to split a fixed compute budget between model size and data. Their answer: scale both roughly equally, which works out to about **20 training tokens per parameter** at the compute-optimal point. Earlier large models were undertrained by that standard. Gopher (280B parameters, 300B tokens) was beaten by Chinchilla (70B parameters, 1.4T tokens) trained with similar compute. The paper fits loss as:

```text
L(N, D) = E + A / N^α + B / D^β
E = irreducible loss of the data; the other terms shrink with size and data
```

"Compute-optimal" means optimal for training cost alone. Once a model is deployed, inference cost depends only on its size, so a smaller model trained far longer than Chinchilla-optimal is cheaper over its lifetime if it reaches the quality you need. Recent small open models are deliberately over-trained: Llama 3 8B was trained on about 15T tokens, close to 2,000 tokens per parameter. Loss keeps improving past the Chinchilla point, just with diminishing returns per FLOP.

Data limits are the other constraint. Muennighoff et al. (2023) found that repeating data for up to about four epochs is nearly as good as fresh data, with returns falling off quickly after that. That is why synthetic data and better filtering of existing data get so much attention.

This runnable calculator applies the 6ND rule and the 20-tokens-per-parameter heuristic, including the lifetime trade-off and a fine-tuning estimate:

```python
import math

PEAK_BF16 = 989e12   # approx. dense BF16 FLOP/s of one H100 SXM (spec sheet)


def train_flops(params: float, tokens: float) -> float:
    return 6 * params * tokens            # 2 forward + 4 backward


def gpu_hours(flops: float, peak: float = PEAK_BF16, mfu: float = 0.4) -> float:
    return flops / (peak * mfu) / 3600


def chinchilla_optimal(compute: float, tokens_per_param: float = 20.0):
    """C = 6 N D with D = r N  =>  N = sqrt(C / (6 r)), D = r N."""
    n = math.sqrt(compute / (6 * tokens_per_param))
    return n, tokens_per_param * n


def lifetime_flops(params: float, train_tokens: float,
                   served_tokens: float) -> float:
    return train_flops(params, train_tokens) + 2 * params * served_tokens


if __name__ == "__main__":
    c = train_flops(70e9, 1.4e12)                    # Chinchilla's own setup
    n, d = chinchilla_optimal(c)
    print(f"{c:.2e} FLOPs -> optimal N={n / 1e9:.0f}B, D={d / 1e12:.1f}T")
    assert abs(n - 70e9) / 70e9 < 0.01 and abs(d - 1.4e12) / 1.4e12 < 0.01

    c8 = train_flops(8e9, 15e12)                     # over-trained 8B model
    print(f"8B on 15T tokens: {c8:.2e} FLOPs, "
          f"{gpu_hours(c8):,.0f} GPU-hours at 40% MFU")
    assert 4.5e5 < gpu_hours(c8) < 5.5e5

    big = lifetime_flops(70e9, 1.4e12, served_tokens=20e12)
    small = lifetime_flops(8e9, 15e12, served_tokens=20e12)
    print(f"lifetime incl. 20T served tokens: 70B {big:.2e}, 8B {small:.2e}")
    assert small < big / 2

    ft = train_flops(8e9, 100e6)                     # full fine-tune, 100M tokens
    print(f"fine-tune 8B on 100M tokens: {gpu_hours(ft):.1f} GPU-hours")
    assert gpu_hours(ft) < 5
```

The numbers to remember: an 8B model on 15T tokens is around half a million H100-hours at 40% MFU; serving 20T tokens afterwards makes the over-trained 8B model less than a third of the lifetime compute of a 70B Chinchilla-optimal model; and a full fine-tune of an 8B model on 100M tokens is a few GPU-hours of compute (memory, not FLOPs, is the hard part, see [Supervised fine-tuning]({{ '/llm/supervised-fine-tuning/' | relative_url }})).

## Training stages

Pretraining produces a base model. Everything you call through a chat API went through several more stages:

```text
pretrain ──► mid-train ──► SFT ──► preference tuning ──► (RL on
trillions    billions of   chat     RLHF, DPO              verifiable
of tokens    curated       demos                           rewards)
             tokens
 base model ─────────────────────► instruct / chat / reasoning model
```

| Stage | Data | Rough scale | What it changes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pretraining | Filtered web, code, books, math | 10^12–10^13 tokens | Knowledge, language, general skills |
| Mid-training (annealing) | Higher-quality and domain data, long documents | 10^10–10^12 tokens | Sharper skills, longer context, fresher data |
| Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) | Prompt and ideal-response pairs in the chat template | 10^4–10^6 examples | Instruction following, format, tool-call syntax |
| Preference tuning | Chosen vs rejected responses, or a reward model | 10^4–10^6 comparisons | Helpfulness, tone, refusals, safety |
| RL with verifiable rewards | Problems with checkable answers (math, code tests) | Many rollouts | Long chain-of-thought reasoning |

- **Mid-training** is a newer name for a phase near the end of pretraining: the learning rate is decayed while the mixture shifts to the highest-quality data, and context length is extended with long documents. Gains from this phase are large relative to its token count.
- **SFT** teaches the chat template and the shape of a good answer. It is the same next-token loss, usually computed only on assistant tokens. See [Supervised fine-tuning]({{ '/llm/supervised-fine-tuning/' | relative_url }}).
- **Preference tuning** optimizes for responses people (or a judge model) prefer. RLHF trains a reward model on comparisons and optimizes the policy against it with PPO (Ouyang et al., 2022). DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) optimizes directly on preference pairs without a separate reward model or RL loop. See [Preference tuning]({{ '/llm/preference-tuning/' | relative_url }}).
- **RL with verifiable rewards** trains on tasks where correctness can be checked automatically. It produced the current generation of reasoning models, which spend many tokens thinking before answering. GRPO, introduced in the DeepSeekMath paper (Shao et al., 2024), is a widely used algorithm here. See [Reasoning models]({{ '/llm/reasoning-models/' | relative_url }}).

## Base vs instruct models

A base model continues documents. Given `What is the capital of France?`, it may answer, or it may continue with three more quiz questions, because that is a plausible continuation of a web page. It responds to framing: `Q: What is the capital of France?\nA:` works because the prompt looks like a document where an answer comes next.

| | Base model | Instruct or chat model |
|---|---|---|
| Behavior | Continues text | Follows instructions in a chat template |
| Prompting | Document framing, few-shot examples | System and user messages |
| Stopping | Runs until max tokens unless the format implies an end | Emits end-of-turn tokens |
| Output diversity | High | Narrower; post-training concentrates probability on preferred styles |
| Calibration | Token probabilities closer to real frequencies | Often less calibrated after preference tuning |
| Safety behavior | Little or none | Refusals and policies trained in |

The calibration point has practical weight: the GPT-4 technical report showed the pretrained model's confidence on a multiple-choice benchmark tracking its accuracy closely and post-training reducing that calibration. If you plan to use logprobs as confidence scores, measure calibration on your own data; see [Decoding and sampling]({{ '/llm/decoding-and-sampling/' | relative_url }}).

When a base model is the right choice: as the starting point for heavy domain fine-tuning where you control the format, for scoring text likelihood (perplexity, anomaly detection), for code completion and fill-in-the-middle, and for generating diverse synthetic data. For almost everything else, start from the instruct model.

## The emergent abilities debate

Wei et al. (2022), "Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models," catalogued tasks where performance stayed near chance as models grew, then jumped sharply above some scale. Schaeffer et al. (2023), "Are Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models a Mirage?", argued that many jumps come from the metric: all-or-nothing scores such as exact match turn smooth improvement in per-token probability into a cliff. With continuous metrics, most of the same curves are smooth.

The practical takeaway does not depend on who is right. Do not extrapolate a capability from a benchmark curve or a model's size; test the specific capability you need on models you can afford, with a metric that gives partial credit where that is meaningful.

## Data contamination

A benchmark is contaminated when its test items, or close paraphrases, appear in training data. Scores then measure memorization. With web-scale corpora, popular benchmarks published online leak into crawls, often through copies on forums and repositories.

How it is detected and mitigated:

- **N-gram overlap** between benchmark items and the training corpus (the GPT-3 paper used 13-gram overlap), followed by removal or reporting of clean subsets.
- **Canary strings.** Some benchmarks embed a unique string so that its presence in a corpus, or a model's ability to complete it, reveals training on the data.
- **Perturbation tests.** A model that scores much worse on paraphrased or re-numbered versions of benchmark items than on the originals likely memorized them.
- **Time-split evaluation.** Questions created after a model's training cutoff cannot be in its training data.
- **Private holdouts.** Test sets never published cannot leak.

For your own work, the lesson is about your eval sets. Keep them private, out of public repositories and out of prompts sent to providers that may train on your data. Check fine-tuning data for overlap with eval data before every run. Treat leaderboard numbers as a shortlist filter, never as a decision. See [LLM evaluation]({{ '/llm/llm-evaluation/' | relative_url }}).

## Pitfalls

- **Prompting a base model like a chat model.** Symptom: rambling, continuing the question, never stopping. Fix: use the instruct variant, or frame the prompt as a document with few-shot examples and a stop sequence.
- **Assuming the model knows recent or private facts.** Symptom: confident, outdated or invented answers. Fix: retrieval and explicit grounding instructions.
- **Choosing by parameter count.** A well-trained smaller model can beat an older larger one. Compare on your task, not on size.
- **Trusting public benchmarks.** Contamination and benchmark-specific tuning inflate scores. Fix: run your own eval on held-out, private data.
- **Extrapolating capability from scale.** Fix: test the actual capability at the size you will deploy.
- **Budgeting fine-tuning by FLOPs alone.** Fine-tuning compute is usually small; GPU memory, data preparation and evaluation dominate cost and time.

## Rules of thumb

- Training FLOPs ≈ `6 × parameters × tokens`; inference ≈ `2 × parameters` per token.
- Compute-optimal training is about 20 tokens per parameter; production models are often trained on 100x more to cut inference cost.
- Data quality and mixture matter as much as size.
- Base models continue text; instruct models follow a template. Know which one you are calling.
- Post-training improves usefulness and can reduce calibration and diversity.
- Treat any public benchmark as possibly contaminated.

## Interview questions

- **What is the pretraining objective, and why does it produce general capabilities?** Minimize cross-entropy of the next token over a huge corpus. Predicting text well requires modeling grammar, facts, code structure and reasoning patterns, so those capabilities emerge as a byproduct.
- **Where does `6ND` come from?** About 2 FLOPs per parameter per token for the forward pass (multiply and add) and about twice that for the backward pass, which computes gradients for both activations and weights.
- **What did the Chinchilla paper show?** For a fixed compute budget, parameters and training tokens should grow roughly equally, about 20 tokens per parameter. Many earlier models were too large for their data; a 70B model on 1.4T tokens beat a 280B model on 300B tokens at similar compute.
- **Why are small models trained far beyond Chinchilla-optimal?** Chinchilla minimizes training compute. Inference cost scales with model size and is paid on every token served, so a smaller, longer-trained model is cheaper over its lifetime.
- **How do base and instruct models differ?** Base models continue documents; instruct models went through SFT and preference tuning to follow a chat template, stop cleanly, refuse some requests and prefer helpful styles, at some cost to calibration and diversity.
- **What is the emergent abilities debate?** Some capabilities appear to jump at a scale threshold. A counterargument shows many jumps come from discontinuous metrics, with smooth improvement under continuous metrics.
- **What is data contamination and how do you guard against it?** Test items in training data inflate scores. Detect with n-gram overlap, canaries, paraphrase tests and time-split evals; guard by keeping private eval sets and checking fine-tuning data for overlap.
